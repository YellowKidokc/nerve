## Central claim

## Best concise argument

3. Therefore, any configuration that possesses meaning must possess some non-random, organized structure—i.e., coherence.
4. Consequently, meaningfulness necessitates coherence as a logical precondition.

## Strongest objection and negative controls
**Objection:** Meaning is observer-relative and can be imposed on any configuration. A random inkblot (Rorschach test) or star pattern is found meaningful by an observer, thus violating the necessity of pre-existing structural coherence. The axiom confuses syntactic structure with semantic meaning.
**Counterexample:** A truly random sequence of bits could, by sheer coincidence, form a valid English sentence when interpreted as ASCII, and thus be "meaningful" despite its generative source being incoherent.
**Competing Interpretation:** Pragmatic or phenomenological philosophies of meaning argue meaning arises from use, context, or consciousness, not intrinsic configuration properties.
**Test Preventing False Conclusion:** Distinguish between *intended* or *inherent* meaning and *ascribed* or *interpreted* meaning. The axiom may only hold for the former, which is much narrower.
