## At a glance
This is a canonical axiom record (A1.2 Distinction) from a formal foundational system. It matters because it posits a primitive condition for describability and knowability. The one-sentence finding is: For any entity to be describable or knowable, it must be distinguishable from at least one other entity.

## Central claim
The exact ontological rule is: ∀x∀y(x≠y → Distinguishable(x,y)). For anything to be describable or knowable, it must be distinguishable from something else.

2.  Premise: To describe or know something is to identify it.
3.  Step: Identification requires a boundary or contrast that separates the thing from what it is not.
4.  Conclusion: Therefore, any describable or knowable thing must be distinguishable from something else.

## Strongest objection and negative controls
**Best Opposing Case:** Advaita Vedanta (particularly Śaṅkara's formulation) asserts that ultimate reality (`Brahman`) is non-dual (`advaita`), without internal distinctions or a true subject-object distinction. In this view, the highest knowledge (`jnana`) is a direct, unmediated realization of this non-distinct unity. Describability and conventional knowability are seen as products of ignorance (`maya`), not requirements of reality itself. The axiom mistakenly elevates a condition of *phenomenal experience* to a condition of *ontology*.
**Counterexample/Test:** The meditative or mystical experience of pure, undifferentiated awareness, often reported across traditions, claims to be a direct knowing without a distinguishable subject knowing a distinguishable object. A test to prevent false conclusions is to rigorously separate epistemological requirements (how we come to know) from ontological claims (what fundamentally is).

## What survives
After considering the non-dual objection, the axiom remains defensible as a necessary condition for *communicable description* and *propositional knowledge*. Even if a non-dual reality exists, any attempt to *speak about it*, *model it*, or *distinguish it in thought* from other concepts (like ignorance) implicitly reinstates distinction. The axiom's scope can be narrowed to "anything that can enter into a descriptive or logical framework," which is the domain the formal system appears to be constructing.

## What this does not establish
This axiom does **not** establish that distinction is a necessary feature of ultimate reality or being-in-itself (ontology). It does **not** prove that non-dual experiences are impossible or incoherent. It does **not** provide a formal derivation of logic or mathematics; it is a starting point. Furthermore, it does **not** earn the conclusion that everything that exists must be distinguishable; A1.1 (Existence) is separate and does not contain this requirement. The move from "describable/knowable" to "existent" is not made here.
